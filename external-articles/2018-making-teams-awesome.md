Making Teams Awesome

Testers are in the business of breaking illusions through identifying and amplifying feedback.
With the feedback, we expect a reaction that makes things better. We improve our products by
identifying problems and by helping make choices on which ones to address first. We improve
our processes by identifying beliefs that could be different, and trying out different ways of doing
things. We improve the people working, amplifying the good and constructively working towards
continuous learning.

People - all of us working in the teams - are the most valuable asset. If people are learning and
growing in various aspects, we stand a change in the world of software that is in constant flux.
In this article addresses a few core beliefs behind learning together.

There’s five core lessons I distill on making teams awesome:
   1.​ Safety is a prerequisite for learning
   2.​ Learning is a superpower
   3.​ Wanting credit can get in the way
   4.​ Understand your contribution
   5.​ Power of teams

Software is for the people, from the people

I like to think of software (product) development as a process of transforming ideas into code.
There’s many shapes of it in how people work together, but it catches the essential: Software is
made by people, and people making it define much of its greatness. The best of software is
made for people, and actually helps them. In words of Kathy Sierra: “We aren’t here to make a
great product or a great company, but rather to make our customers awesome at whatever they
do with our products or services.“

The people-centric view to software leads us quickly to the idea of when people are at their best
- what makes people awesome? Recent findings from Google Project Aristoteles have shown
that the key to a high-performing team is
psychological safety - the ability to be
vulnerable in front of others with our
diverse viewpoints, to take risks and
trust that everything will be ok. At times
we think of the working culture as
something given or as something
management creates, yet each of us has
a role to play in creating it. Each of us
can take the initiative in building better
relationships between testers, test automators and developers.

An Industry of Learning

People at the core, we come to the idea of learning, both individually and together. Those who
are learning win over those who are not. Let’s play a little thought experiment.

Imagine you could invest time to become 1 percent better each day. It’s like interest that
compounds, each day. How much time would you need to invest and how quickly could that
investment pay back? First, using time on learning and not just doing work would slow you
down. Eventually, it would make you faster. What timescales are we thinking of here?

If you used 1 hour a day to get 1 percent improvement, you would be ahead of those not
learning in 28 days as your learning makes you faster. You could use as much as 5.5 hours of a
full 8 hour working day to achieve that 1 percent learning and still be ahead of those not learning
in a year. Learning is like a superpower.

That superpower does not come automatically, but we actually have to work around our biases
and perceptions to get to it. Some of my most valuable lessons originally looked like bad ideas
to me. Experimenting, allowing ourselves
to do things we logically believe must be
wrong can play a key role. When we’re
doing things we inherently don’t want to
believe in, we may learn about cognitive
dissonance. Cognitive dissonance
reduction is the idea that when our actions
and beliefs are in conflict, they change to
be in sync. Give yourself a chance of
changing your mind - learning - through
experiencing things.

Crediting in Teamwork

Wanting to be recognized and remembered for the good work we’re doing is a basic need of
people. In software, just like in many other fields, crediting is a hard problem. If you think of
making a cake, can you attribute which of the many ingredients was the one making the cake
excellent? If you did not have sugar, surely the cake isn’t good? But it’s the same with flour, or
any of the ingredients we use in smaller amounts. It’s the sum of the ingredients that makes the
cake great. It’s the sum of the people, working in collaboration, that makes software great.

A famous case in difficulty of crediting is Albert Einstein. There’s been relevant research into the
contributions of his first wife, Mileva Maric Einstein, a physicist who contributed significantly to
his groundbreaking science on theory of relativity. Many of us know from our own experiences
that there are moments when it’s hard to say who really came up with a particular idea. It is also
questionable if the idea is worth more than following through to delivering something based on
the idea.

Similarly interesting research was done on the idea of why there are no baby dinosaurs. A
human need of crediting lead to a point where 5/12 identified dinosaurs did not exist, but were in
fact just younger versions of the already identified species. People have a strong need of
contribution, to a degree that they are willing to dismiss potential other narratives.

In software development, best ideas win when we care about work over credit and follow
through and finishing are just as relevant as coming up with an idea in the first place. It takes a
village to build awesome software.

Telling the Story of Tester Contributions

On our path to “team awesome”, we need to discuss “tester awesome.” We all have our unique
traits that be bring into the work we are doing, crafting ourselves into the job.

I’ve come to realize I can describe my identity with stories of the four C’s. I’m a Catalyst, a
Conscience, a Cheerleader and a Critical Thinker.

As a catalyst, I make slow reactions faster through feedback tailored for the time and place. I
start to change things without asking for permission. Asking for forgiveness after, and only if
needed, is a powerful rule of thumb. My power of change includes myself and my environment.
Others may change in reaction to the environment changing, but forcing change on others have
usually not been the best of ideas.

As a conscience, I hold space for testing and quality to happen. Sometimes the developers in
my teams do great testing just because when they look at me, they remember what I’ve stood
for in the past. I can bring in the sense of right and wrong, good and bad, by choosing what
feedback to focus on, what to amplify.

As a cheerleader, I remember that staying
positive and turning up the good are within
my reach. I can actively look for positive
feedback, and always seek to strike a
balance in my product critique. And no
matter what messages I deliver, being
authentic is key.

As a critical thinker I see things that others
don’t. I use the product as my external
imagination to think critically about how well it works, identifying a variety of problems others
miss. I can use my critical thinking skills on ways of working, realizing all the variables we can
tweak to make things better.

From Me to My Team

Putting all of this together, core to
understanding is that each of us, as testers,
are one relevant piece in a team. In the last
years, we’ve learned how to bring testing from
activity in the end to a continuous activity.
We’ve learned to focus testing efforts into time
before implementation. And we’ve learned that
a strong testing presence is possible even
while developing through mechanisms such as
pairing and mobbing.

Teams can do things individuals cannot. Teams
can take the best of every one of us into the
work we’re doing. Teams can become awesome in ways that make users awesome. The scale
of what a team can do is more than what any of us can do alone. Give everyone access to the
tools and systems and you find you’ll be surprised with the extra contributions those with added
abilities can make.

What are you doing differently tomorrow to make your team more awesome? Start by speaking
up about it with your team. Enthusiasm is infectious.
